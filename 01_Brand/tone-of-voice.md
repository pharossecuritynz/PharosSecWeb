# Tone of Voice

## Voice in one sentence

Pharos Security sounds like a calm, experienced advisor sitting across the table from a business owner — not a vendor, not a technician, not a scaremonger.

## The five voice attributes

| Attribute | We sound like | We don't sound like |
|---|---|---|
| **Calm** | "Here's what matters most, and why." | "You could be breached tomorrow!" |
| **Plain-English** | "This means someone could read your emails without you knowing." | "This exposes you to unauthorised lateral movement via credential compromise." |
| **Practical** | "Here are the three things worth doing this quarter." | "Here is a 40-point framework covering every conceivable control." |
| **Honest** | "This isn't urgent yet — revisit it next year." | Implying everything is equally critical to justify more work. |
| **Warm but professional** | "Let's walk through where you're exposed." | Overly casual slang, emojis, forced friendliness, or stiff corporate-speak. |

## Writing rules

1. **Lead with meaning, not mechanism.** Say what a risk means for the business before (or instead of) explaining how it technically works.
2. **One idea per sentence.** Cyber topics are already unfamiliar to most readers — don't compound that with complex sentence structure.
3. **Avoid unexplained acronyms.** If a term like "MFA" or "SPF" is necessary, define it in plain language on first use.
4. **No manufactured urgency.** Never use countdown language, breach statistics as scare tactics, or "act now" pressure.
5. **No absolutes unless true.** Avoid "guarantee," "100% secure," "unhackable," or "bulletproof." Cyber security reduces risk; it doesn't eliminate it — say so.
6. **Address the business owner, not the IT department.** Even when content is technical, write for a reader without a security background, unless a specific deliverable is explicitly written for an IT provider.
7. **Show respect for what they've already done.** Most SMEs have done *something*. Acknowledge existing effort before pointing out gaps.

## Words we use

Clear, practical, plain-English, independent, priorities, proportionate, steady, confidence, everyday habits, right-sized, straightforward.

## Words we avoid

Hacker, cutting-edge, bulletproof, military-grade, unbreakable, best-in-class, world-class, weaponised, threat actors (prefer "attackers" or "people trying to access your systems"), synergy, leverage (as a verb), disrupt.

## Example: same message, two ways

**Not on-brand (fear-based, jargon-heavy):**
> "Cybercriminals are targeting SMEs like never before. Without immediate action, your business could suffer a catastrophic breach. Our military-grade phishing simulation platform will harden your human firewall."

**On-brand (calm, plain, practical):**
> "Email is still the most common way small businesses get compromised. We'll show you where your current setup is exposed and the three changes that would make the biggest difference — most of which can be done in a week."

## Tone across formats

| Format | Tone notes |
|---|---|
| Website | Confident, warm, slightly more polished/aspirational |
| Reports | Precise, structured, plain-English explanations before technical detail |
| Emails | Personable, direct, no filler |
| LinkedIn | Professional but human; share genuine insight, not engagement-bait |
| In-person/calls | Conversational version of the same principles — listen first, avoid jargon, check understanding |
